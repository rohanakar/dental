dcad · how one algorithm grew up

Finding the finish line

A crown is built on one closed line around a prepared tooth. This is how dcad learned to find that line on a 3D scan. It started as a shortest path a hand could steer. It became a cut through the scan that reads the tooth's shape the way a technician's fingertip does. Each step came from a failure, and the failures are where the ideas are.

01the problem

The line a crown is built on

Where exactly does the tooth end and the crown begin?

To fit a crown, a dentist grinds the tooth down into a prep: a tapered stump with a ledge around its base. The ledge is the finish line. The crown's edge, the margin, must sit exactly on it. Too short and the tooth decays under the gap. Too long and the edge overhangs and irritates the gum. Labs work to about a tenth of a millimetre, and dcad's own checks to a few hundredths.

On a plaster model a technician finds the line by feel and marks it with a red pencil. dcad gets a scan instead: a mesh of up to a million triangles, in millimetres, with no labels. Nothing in the file says prep, wall or gum. Everything has to be read from the shape.

Before you read on

Run a fingertip down the cut in the figure above, from the top of the prep to the gum. What does it feel at the margin that it feels nowhere else?

Not the steepest part, not the highest part. Think about which way the surface bends, and in what order.

A sequence. A long steep wall, then a small concave inner angle (the floor of the chamfer), then a sharp convex corner, the lip, then the surface drops away again toward the gum. The finish line is that convex corner. Every version of the algorithm below is a better way of finding "the convex corner after the wall", and of not being fooled by the other convex corners on a tooth.

Hold on to that sequence: wall, inner angle, lip, drop. It returns in chapter 10 almost word for word.

02Day 2 · 11 Oct 2026

Let the hand lead

A technician will click a few points. How do you join them along the finish line, not straight across the tooth?

The first tool was manual on purpose. The user clicks a handful of points around the prep, and the software draws the line between them. Join the clicks with straight segments and you cut across the tooth. Join them with the shortest path on the surface (a geodesic) and you still drift off the ledge between clicks.

Before you read on

You will run a shortest-path search over the mesh's edges. What should one edge cost, so that the cheapest path between two clicks hugs the finish line?

Chapter 1 says the finish line is a convex ridge. Make walking along convex edges nearly free. And what should walking through a groove cost?

Length times a factor of the edge's signed curvature κ̄ (the mean of its two vertices', convex positive, measured on smoothed normals). With κ₀ = 1 mm⁻¹:

κ̄ > 0 : cost = length · max(0.02, 1 / (1 + (κ̄/κ₀)²)) // a ridge costs as little as 1/50 κ̄ ≤ 0 : cost = length · (1 + min(2, −κ̄/κ₀)) // a groove up to 3×

Then plain Dijkstra from each click, bounded to 12 mm. The whole predecessor tree goes to the screen, so the preview line to the cursor is a lookup, not a search. This is the classic live-wire or "intelligent scissors" from image editing, moved onto a curved surface.

Measured, synthetic prep

Four clicks: the curvature-weighted path is 0.077 mm from the true line on average. A plain geodesic between the same clicks is 0.305 mm off. Eight clicks on a million-triangle arch: 0.055 mm mean, 0.129 mm max.

Fixed on the way

The first synthetic test tooth had no convex margin at all. Its only convex edge was the line angle at the top of the walls, so the tracer was learning the wrong lesson. The test prep was rebuilt with a chamfer and a convex lip. The spec put the target in one line: snap to the ridge, not the chamfer floor.

0327 Oct 2026

A pixel off

One click landed 0.137 mm off the line, and the crown could not be printed.

The live-wire goes exactly where it is told: through every click. On the demo tooth, seven scripted clicks sat within 0.063 mm of the ridge and one sat 0.137 mm down the chamfer. The trace dipped 0.24 mm to reach it and came back, turning 44°, 56° and 171° within 0.2 mm. The crown's edge band followed that spike, folded over itself, and the crown was no longer a valid solid.

Why only now? The demo jaw had just been given realistic gum, which sharpened the curvature around the margin enough to turn a small dip into a fold. A human click is no more precise than a screen pixel, so this was a product bug, not bad luck.

Before you read on

You will move each click onto the ridge before tracing. But a click that is already on the ridge must not slide along it. What rule does both?

A ridge is broad on a mesh: neighbouring vertices along it are nearly as strong as each other. Compare the click to the best nearby, not to zero.

Look within 0.3 mm along the surface for the vertex with the strongest curvature of the line's sign. Move the click there only if the clicked vertex scores under four-fifths of it. On the ridge, the click already scores close to the best, so it stays. A first version snapped whenever anything stronger was near, and crept along the ridge. Drags are deliberately not snapped: a drag is a placement.

Try it in the figure above. The example clicks sit about 0.15 mm off the ridge. With the snap off, the line kinks at each one; with it on, the dotted stubs show each click moving onto the ridge. The unit test that guards this: a click 0.15 mm down the chamfer ends 0.072 mm from the ridge instead of 0.199 mm, and the sharpest turn drops from 40.6° to 30.5°.

04Day 6 · Oct 2026

Let the machine click

One-click crown design needs a margin with no clicks at all.

The tracer was good. What a one-click design lacks is the clicks. So the first automatic margin did only that: it placed the clicks and let the live-wire do the rest.

Before you read on

You know the tooth's centre and its up direction. How would you find 16 points on the finish line, all the way round?

Walk outward from the centre in 16 directions, like spokes. Along each spoke, what do you look for first?

Sixteen radial height profiles: along each spoke, cast rays straight down from above every 0.05 mm and record the height of the first surface hit. On each profile take the first strong convex break after the height has dropped at least 1.5 mm (the wall), stop if the surface rises again (a neighbouring tooth), and hand the 16 points to the live-wire as clicks.

Then a gate decides whether the line may be used without a human: it must close, be 8–60 mm long, and have no turn sharper than 120°. On the synthetic arch the automatic margin landed 0.048 mm from the truth on average, slightly better than the hand-scripted clicks at 0.055 mm. That happy result would not survive contact with real teeth, but it took three weeks and real scans to find out.

Tried and dropped
  • Scoring corners by vertex curvature. The curvature was negative exactly on the convex margin edge: scanners leave sliver triangles there, and their normals swing the wrong way. The profile's own change of slope replaced it.
  • Taking the first convex peak. A diagonal spoke between two teeth picked the gum in the gap. The peak must now reach half of the profile's strongest.
  • Measuring kinks between 0.2 mm segments. It rejected a 0.057 mm-accurate line, because snapping to vertices makes 0.2 mm wiggles that look like kinks. Turns are measured over 0.6 mm chords instead. A doubled-back 1 mm spike still fails.
0526 Oct 2026

Don't trust one trace

The automatic margin passed its own checks while sitting 1.7 mm off the finish line.

A user wrote that one-click design "always seems to fail at margins". On a synthetic benchmark of 17 prepped teeth with scanner-like noise, the plain automatic margin was right on 6. Worse, some wrong lines passed the gate: smooth, closed, the right length, and 1.71 mm up the wall.

Before you read on

Shape checks can't see a wrong-but-smooth line. The line is traced through 16 anchors. What one ratio, free to compute, betrays a trace that took detours between them?

If the anchors were all on the true line, how long would the trace be, compared with the polygon through the anchors?

Wander: the traced length divided by the perimeter of the anchor polygon. A detour adds length; a good trace is barely longer than its anchor ring. dcad's limit is 1.15, and the gate gained a "no detours" check. On natural teeth it reads 1.37–1.64, which is how the refiner rejects a tooth that was never prepared.

The refiner stopped trusting a single trace. Each profile now reports every strong convex corner, not just the first. Profiles come in four counts (16, 24, 32, 48). Anchors are chosen three ways: the first corners, corners consistent round the ring, and a ring dynamic program that picks one corner per profile so that the height and radius change slowly around the tooth. Each anchor set is traced twice, about 14 traces in 0.2–0.7 s, and the best is kept: passes the gate, then least wander, then the gentlest turn.

Tried and dropped: a language model in the loop

A model was given margin tools (move an anchor, re-run, smooth) and a budget of ten calls. The ground rule was geometry is computed, never generated: the model only chose which tool to call. It added nothing the refiner didn't already do. It was right on the cases the refiner got right, still 0.66 mm off on the one it missed, and took minutes. Handed a passing report, it finished at once: "from numbers alone it cannot tell a gum ledge from the finish line". It was removed from one-click design.

Measured, synthetic benchmark

Prepped teeth right (within 0.15 mm mean and passing the gate): plain 6 of 17, refiner 16 of 17. Natural teeth rejected: 3 of 3 for both.

0627 Oct 2026

A detour to the gum line

On a tooth that was never prepared, the line that matters is where tooth meets gum, and the finish-line finder made stairs of it.

Some restorations sit on natural teeth, and there the useful line is the gum line: a concave crease, the opposite sign of a finish line. The finish-line finder followed it in 39–54 mm staircases. Measured on real natural teeth from a public research dataset (Teeth3DS+, local evaluation only), it was right on 0 of 24 held-out teeth, with a median error of 1.50 mm.

A research round ran a proposer, a scout, a critic and four experiments in parallel. The winner did not follow the crease at all. It let the crease stop a flow: a random walker. Fix a potential of 1 on the crown's top and 0 on the rim of a patch around it, give every mesh edge a conductance that collapses on concave creases, solve for the steady state, and draw the 0.5 level line.

conductance(edge) = exp(−β · concavity / κ_ref) / length // κ_ref: the patch's 90th percentile
Before you read on

Slide β below. Correct teeth jump from 0 to 20 of 23 and then stop improving. What does a plateau like that tell you about the method?

Where does the potential drop from 1 to 0 when every edge conducts the same? And when the crease conducts e−8 as well as the rest?

That the crease, not the seeds, places the line. With uniform conductance the drop spreads evenly and the 0.5 line sits wherever the seeds put it. Once the crease conducts about e−8 as well as everything else, the whole drop piles up there, and beyond that no further contrast moves it. "A plateau, not a tuned point": β = 8 was not fitted to the data, it is simply past the knee.

Two lessons that shaped everything after

Synthetic and real rankings invert. The method that was best on synthetic teeth (24 of 32) was right on 7 of 24 held-out real teeth. The walker, 16 of 32 on synthetic, was 22 of 24. From then on synthetic data was a regression harness, not a way to choose methods.

The click was an oracle. The benchmark clicked at the label's centre. Moving the click 1.5 mm dropped the walker from 20 to 9 of 23. A probe that walks down the tooth from inside and re-centres on opposite walls fixed it: 83 of 96 jittered clicks right on held-out teeth.

0729 Oct 2026

Meeting real preps

Measured on real lab preps for the first time, the automatic margin was right on 3 of 24.

Everything so far had been measured on synthetic prepared teeth. Then came 60 demo cases that ship with a commercial dental CAD: real lab preps, scanned on lab scanners, with the lab's own margin lines stored in the files. A line counted as right when its mean distance to the lab's line was at most 0.10 mm, with 90% of it within 0.2 mm, covering 90% of the lab's line.

Before you read on

Same tracer, same scans. Clicks on the lab's line give 22 of 24 right, automatic anchors give 3. Where is the bug, and what do you expect the wrong lines to look like?

Look back at the die in the first figure. How many convex corners does a cut cross, from the top down?

The tracer is fine; the anchors are wrong. Most wrong lines follow the lab's line and then climb 1.5–3.5 mm up the prep for a stretch. Several ride the edge where the walls meet the top of the prep the whole way round. The lesson in one sentence from the spec: the finish line is not the sharpest edge on a real prep; the line angle at the top of its walls often is.

Two more numbers from that day set up the next three chapters. The gate passed 39 of 52 wrong lines: shape alone can't tell a line angle from a finish line. And a doubt warning, built for review colouring and not part of the gate, flagged 47 of the 52 wrong lines and none of the 6 right ones. The scan knew more than the gate asked it.

0829 Oct 2026

Cut the scan, don't look down on it

Plotting each profile next to the lab's point showed where the height map went blind.

The profiles were plotted one by one with the lab's point on them. Two things stood out. Real axial walls are 2–5 mm tall, near vertical, often slightly overhanging. Rays from above have nowhere to land on such a wall: the height profile jumps from the line angle straight to the bottom. And on what remains, noise reads as corners, because the convexity score, a change of slope dh/dr, blows up wherever the surface is steep. The pick landed just past the 1.5 mm minimum drop, halfway down the wall.

Where was the lab's point? "Always the first convex corner after the axial wall": the outer lip of the chamfer or shoulder.

Before you read on

Give a definition of "corner" that treats a vertical wall exactly like a flat floor, and say how you would sample a profile that can see under an overhang.

A slope depends on which way is up. What property of a curve doesn't? And instead of asking the scan "how high are you here?", ask it "where do you cross this plane?"

Cut the mesh with a half-plane through the tooth's axis and walk the real cross-section outward from the top, crossing triangle by triangle. Crossings are keyed by mesh edge or vertex, so the chain is exact, and only triangles in grid cells near the plane are visited. The walk stops at the scan's border, past the search radius, if it wraps back past the axis, or on a 0.5 mm rise onto a neighbour.

Measure corners as the contour's turn: resample every 0.05 mm, take the angle between the chords 0.15 mm before and after each point, divide by 30°. A turn doesn't care which way the wall faces. The pick is the first convex corner at least 1.5 mm below the top, and every corner goes to the refiner as a candidate.

How much to smooth?

Noise is the remaining enemy: a rough scan turns everywhere. dcad smooths each profile with a Gaussian, but not by a fixed amount. It measures the profile's roughness, the median turn before smoothing, and picks the least smoothing (σ from 0 to 0.4 mm) that brings the median turn to 6° or less. A quiet scan is left alone, so its corners stay sharp; a rough one is calmed just enough. On scans rougher than 6°, the old height profiles are run too and all candidates compete. That fallback rescued the three roughest synthetic cases and costs nothing on real scans.

The surprise

Real lab scans turned out to be quieter than the synthetic "noisy" ones: 1.3–6° of roughness (median 1.9°) against 9–70°. Real profiles were never smoothed at all. The hard part of real data was shape, not noise.

Evidence, before a line of the new picker was written

Per profile, a convex corner on the true contour lies within 0.2 mm of the lab's point on 90% of tuning profiles and 98% held out; the height map reaches 51% and 68%. The rule "first convex corner at least 1.5 mm below the top" picks right on 87% / 89%, against the old pick's 41% / 49%. Lines right: tuning 3 → 17 of 19, held out 2 → 11 of 17, front teeth (never tuned on) 1 → 12 of 27. A margin takes 0.10–0.32 s.

0929 Oct 2026, round two

Let the scan vote

Each profile was now nearly always right. The lines still went wrong, by choosing among them.

Per profile, the first pick was at its ceiling: 87.8% within 0.2 mm. "Most remaining misses are a line choice, not a profile choice." The refiner traced about twenty candidate lines and ranked them by shape, and a line that follows the line angle for a stretch has a perfectly good shape. Worse, the ring DP preferred sharp corners, and line angles are the sharpest thing on a prep.

Before you read on

You have twenty candidate lines, all smooth. Without any reference line, how could the scan itself say which one sits on a crease and which one only runs beside a stronger one?

Walk across the line, not along it. If you stand on a line and look 1.5 mm to each side, what would you see if you were standing beside the real edge instead of on it?

The doubt check. Every 0.5 mm along the line, march across it on the scan for ±1.5 mm and read the cross-profile's curvature over ±0.15 mm. A point is on its crease when the crease is strong enough there; it is off when a clearly stronger crease of the same sign lies nearby (for finish lines: more than 0.35 mm away and 1.6× stronger). Runs of off points are the doubtful stretches.

On a quiet scan, candidate lines are now ranked by passing the gate, then the fewest off stretches, then wander and turn. The check is lazy: the first passing line with no off stretch wins, so a good case pays for only one check. And a new gate check, margin.doubt, stops one-click on any off stretch, so a doubtful line goes to a human instead of into a crown.

The ring figure in chapter 5 marks doubt the same way, per spoke: a chosen corner is in doubt (a dashed ring) when a crease at least 1.6× stronger lies 0.35–1.5 mm from it. Make the wall corners run all the way round, sharp, until the ring DP takes them. Every spoke lights up as doubtful, because the line angle runs just above the chosen corner, and the gate stops one-click. On the finish line nothing is flagged.

Considered and not done
  • A structural per-profile rule (skip a corner with a shallow surface before it and a wall after it, as a line angle has): 89.1% against 87.8%, not worth its cost on noisy synthetic scans.
  • Relative-strength thresholds: they traded front teeth against back teeth.
  • Changing the DP's taste for sharp corners: the line-level numbers gave no reason to.

The rules for this round: the round-one held-out set had been looked at, so it no longer counted as held out. Every change needed a geometric reason, and no back-tooth case that was right was allowed to turn wrong.

Measured

Back teeth: 28 → 30 of 36 right; the gate now passes 2 of 6 wrong lines and stops 1 of 30 right ones. Front teeth: 12 → 14 of 27, and a frame fix the next day (part of the "margin" failure on front teeth was the tooth's up direction pointing into the tooth) took them to 19 of 27. No case turned wrong. Each newly right case "picked a doubt-free line over a shape-preferred one beside the line angle".

1030 Oct 2026

The same cut finds the prep

Before any margin, something has to say which tooth on the scan is prepared at all.

dcad's prep finder looks over the whole jaw for prepared teeth, so frames and margins can start without a click. It used height profiles too, with the same blind spot on vertical walls: on real preps only 3 of 26 reached its threshold, and natural cusps sometimes did.

The fix was chapter 1's fingertip, written as a rule on the true sections, shared with the margin's code:

wall ≥ 1 mm of drop steeper than 50° inner angle a concave turn of at least 8° lip a convex turn of at least 12°, within 1.5 mm drop then at least 0.4 mm more a prep: at least 65% of 24 sections around a peak show it

The cut figure in chapter 8 marks this signature on its contour: the teal dot where the wall ends, the amber ring at the inner angle, the red dot at the lip.

Before you read on

The threshold of 0.65 finds 18 of 26 real back-tooth preps. Lower it a little and you would find more of the misses: low, wide molars with almost no wall. Why not lower it?

What happens downstream when the finder rings a tooth that was never prepared, compared with missing one that was?

The highest natural peak scores 0.58. A wall-free variant caught the low molars only by accepting corners of 20° or less, and then it ringed 2–3 natural peaks. A missed prep costs the user one click; a false one designs a crown on a healthy tooth. "A wrong ring is worse than none." And neighbouring settings (a 40° wall, a 4–8° turn, a lip within 2 mm) gave the same counts: a plateau again, not a fitted point. False preps on 28 natural jaws went from 15 to 0.

12as of 30 Oct 2026

Still open

Six back teeth of 36 are still wrong. Here is why, and what is not yet known.

  • Margins that swing in height round the tooth. A premolar on a short segment scan whose margin rises 5 mm from one side to the other. The ring DP assumes the height changes slowly.
  • Short proximal walls. Between teeth the margin can sit only 1.1–1.5 mm below the prep's top, under the 1.5 mm minimum drop.
  • Deep margins. The cut reaches 10 mm below the top; some telescope crowns go deeper.
  • Wall-free preps. Low, wide molars with almost no wall are not found by the prep finder, by choice.
  • Doubt on rough scans. It is trusted only on quiet scans; on noisy synthetic ones it flags half the right lines.
  • Overfitting. Every real back-tooth case has now been looked at. The true held-out test will be a lab's own cases, measured once.
13for you

Invent it yourself

The algorithm, as the questions that produce it, in order.

  1. What does a fingertip feel at the margin? A wall, a concave inner angle, a convex lip, a drop. The margin is that convex corner.
  2. How do you join clicks along it? Shortest paths where convex edges are nearly free and grooves cost up to three times their length.
  3. What if a click is a pixel off? Move it to the strongest ridge within 0.3 mm, unless it already scores four-fifths of that.
  4. Who places the clicks? Spokes from the tooth's centre; on each, the first convex corner after the wall.
  5. How do you catch a smooth wrong line? Wander: traced length over the anchor ring's perimeter.
  6. Why trust one trace? Don't: every corner is a candidate; a ring DP keeps neighbours consistent; trace many, keep the best.
  7. Why does it fail on real preps? The line angle is sharper than the finish line, and looking down at a vertical wall sees nothing.
  8. What is a corner that ignores which way is up? The turn of the true cross-section's contour, over ±0.15 mm chords.
  9. How much to smooth? The least that makes the median turn quiet (6°); a quiet scan keeps its sharp corners.
  10. Which candidate line is right? The one the scan agrees sits on its crease: march across the line and look for a stronger crease nearby.
  11. When should a human look? When the line fails the gate, or any stretch of it is in doubt.
  12. How do you know you are not fooling yourself? Real held-out data, ablations like β = 0, and plateaus rather than tuned points.

If you closed the answers and still wrote most of these down, you have reinvented a production margin finder.
